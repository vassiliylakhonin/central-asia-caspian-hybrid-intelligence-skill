## Bottom line

The CKU financing is signed and, per the Kyrgyz government, closed: a $4.7 billion estimated cost, about half as a 35-year loan from a China Development Bank / Eximbank syndicate to the project company, and about half as equity split 51/24.5/24.5 between China, Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan [1][7]. Structurally this is financeable as a state-led project: the borrower is a state-owned joint venture, so traffic and currency risk sit with sovereign shareholders, 51% Chinese, not with an unguaranteed private concessionaire. It is not commercially bankable on public evidence: the revenue basis is an untested promoter forecast of 12–15 million tons per year [5][6]; no tariff policy, traffic study, JV financial statements, or loan pricing are public; and all through-traffic must cross a gauge break at Makmal whose capacity is unpublished [2][5]. The single biggest blocker is demand risk with no contracted traffic, resting ultimately on the shareholder with the least fiscal room: Kyrgyzstan.

## 1. Project and sponsor

- **What is being built / financed:** a single-track railway from Kashgar (China) via Torugart, Makmal and Jalal-Abad (Kyrgyzstan) to Andijan (Uzbekistan). Total length is reported between 486 km (2024 feasibility reporting [8]) and 523–533 km [4][6][7]; the Kyrgyz section is about 304–312 km [1][2][5]. Gauge: 1,435 mm from the Chinese border to Makmal (~160–169.6 km), 1,520 mm from Makmal to Jalal-Abad (~142–146.26 km), with a transshipment and wagon-transfer complex at Makmal [2][4][8]. Structures: official 2025 figures give 50 bridges and 29 tunnels totalling ~120 km, about 40% of the route [1][7]; 2024 feasibility reporting gave 81 bridges (26 km), 41 tunnels (120 km) and 18 stations [8] — unresolved design change or scope difference `[verify]`. Maximum speed 120 km/h [8].
- **Sponsor / promoter and its capacity:** China–Kyrgyzstan–Uzbekistan Railway Company LLC, opened in Bishkek on 28 September 2024; director general Zhou Xin [1][2]. The authorized organizations behind it are China Railway International (a CREC subsidiary), state enterprise NC Kyrgyz Temir Zholu, and Uzbekistan Temir Yollary [8]. The JV is a new entity with no operating history; its financial statements are not public — `Judgment`, `[verify]`.
- **Stage:** construction. Launch ceremony 27 December 2024 [1][7]; construction of key facilities launched 29 April 2025 [2]; the investment agreement's full-financing condition was met by 20 December 2025 per the Kyrgyz government [1]. By early September 2026, work had begun on most tunnels and bridges of the Kyrgyz section, and the first tunnel (Kosh-Dobo North No. 2, 209.6 m) had been bored through [5].
- **Why now:** post-2022 rerouting away from Russian territory revived a project conceived in the 1990s; the trilateral intergovernmental agreement was signed 6 June 2024 and the investment agreement on 20 December 2024 [2][4].

## 2. Revenue and traffic basis

- **How the project earns:** haulage / infrastructure-access charges plus Makmal transshipment fees; no tariff schedule, access-charge framework, or revenue model is published. `Judgment`, `[verify]`.
- **Traffic / demand assumption and its evidence:** promoter forecast of up to 15 million tons per year [6], reported elsewhere as 12–15 million tons [5]; 2024 feasibility reporting cited 13.5 million tons by 2050 [8]. The relationship between these figures (near-term capacity vs long-run forecast) is unresolved `[verify]`. Kyrgyz political revenue claims run at $200 million per year (President Japarov) and $300 million per year (Deputy Prime Minister Baisalov) [9]; Uzbekistan's transit benefit is cited at $150–200 million per year [8]. All are national-economy figures, not JV revenue — `Plausible` as claims, `Unknown` as revenue basis.
- **Benchmarks:** Kazakhstan–China rail freight was 18.7 million tons in the first half of 2026 alone, up 9% year on year [5]; the World Bank projects the entire Middle Corridor Caspian flow tripling to about 11 million tons by 2030 [3]. A single-track mountain line with a gauge break carrying 12–15 million tons would exceed the projected total of the established corridor; the forecast has not been independently modelled in public [5]. `Judgment`.
- **Sensitivity:** demand must be diverted from road transport via Kyrgyzstan and rail via Kazakhstan, plus new China–Middle East flows; the Makmal yard is the physical bottleneck and its design capacity is unpublished [5]. Quantified sensitivity is impossible without the JV traffic model `[verify]`.

## 3. Cost and financing structure

- **Capex and its basis:** $4.7 billion, the official estimate at financial close [1][7]. Conflict: 2024 reporting put the Kyrgyz segment alone at ~$4.7 billion within a possible ~$8 billion total [8]. Whether $4.7 billion is the whole project or the Kyrgyz scope is `[verify]`.
- **Currency exposure:** all public figures are in USD; the JV loan currency, the revenue currency mix, and any hedging are undisclosed `[verify]`.
- **Funding gap:** $4.7bn cost less ~$2.3bn loan less ~$2.3bn equity leaves a ~$100 million rounding residue (calculation M-004). Whether the halves are exactly $2.3bn is `[verify]`.
- **Equity arithmetic conflict:** 24.5% of $2.3bn is $563.5 million; The Diplomat reports $573 million each for Kyrgyzstan and Uzbekistan and $1.1 billion for China [9]; Kyrgyz officials put Kyrgyzstan's share at roughly $609 million [6]. Three mutually inconsistent figures (calculation M-003); unresolved `[verify]`.

## 4. Bankability blockers (the four)

1. **Traffic / demand risk — the binding one.** Demand is a promoter forecast, not contracted traffic, and no public traffic study, tariff policy, or JV financial statements exist to test it [5]. Level annual debt service on the USD 2.3bn, 35-year loan is about USD 92m at 2%, USD 123m at 4%, and USD 159m at 6% (calculation M-001). Against the only public revenue anchors ($150–300m/yr national-level claims, pre-opex), coverage ranges from comfortable to failing: at 6% with the low anchor, debt service is not covered even before operating costs (DSCR 0.95, M-001). The structure only works if the undisclosed loan pricing is deeply concessional and the traffic materializes. `Judgment`.
2. **Currency / FX risk — unquantifiable on public data.** Loan currency and revenue currency mix are undisclosed; Kyrgyz equity is part-funded by USD debt against a som budget `[verify]`.
3. **Regulatory & legal frame — partly documented.** The trilateral investment agreement (20 December 2024) governs the project [2]; Kyrgyz support includes land, customs and tax exemptions, and work-permit waivers [8]. Tariff-setting authority, access-charge regulation, and dispute-resolution terms are unpublished `[verify]`. 214 homes are to be demolished with compensation disputes open, and Kyrgyz regulators have recorded environmental violations on the works [5] — social and environmental friction sits with Bishkek.
4. **Counterparty / sponsor capacity — mixed.** The contractor side is experienced: China Railway International / CREC built two-thirds of China's mainline railways and the China–Laos railway [8] (`Plausible`). The JV itself is new, its financial statements unpublished, and shareholder-support mechanics undisclosed `[verify]`.

## 5. Risk allocation — the critical imbalance, inverted

The region's recurring PPP failure is that states decline long-term traffic and FX guarantees, pushing both onto private sponsors who then cannot finance. CKU avoids that failure mode by having no private sponsor at all: the borrower is a state-owned JV and the lenders are state policy banks. The inversion is that no sovereign guarantees are published either, so a USD 2.3bn loan rides on traffic whose existence is not confirmed by the cited sources as of 2026-11-10; if the JV underperforms, the shareholders will be asked to make up the difference pro rata [5] (`Judgment`). Kyrgyzstan has the least fiscal room of the three: public debt $10.1bn, external debt $5.22bn, Eximbank of China already the largest single creditor at $1.41bn (27.1% of external debt, June 2026), rising above $1.7bn with the new tranche [5][6]. The project is financeable because the 51% shareholder treats it as strategic infrastructure, not because the revenue case clears a commercial bar. That is a viable structure for a state project; it is not evidence a lender could take to credit committee on the public record.

## 6. What would make it bankable

- Publication of the JV traffic study and tariff policy, including Makmal transshipment charges. Resolves blocker 1.
- Anchor-shipper or access agreements for China–Uzbekistan / Tajikistan / Iran flows. Resolves blocker 1.
- Disclosure of the Makmal yard design capacity and operating plan. Resolves the physical-bottleneck unknown behind blocker 1.
- JV audited financial statements and disclosure of the shareholder-support mechanism behind F-001. Resolves blockers 1 and 4.
- Disclosure of F-001 pricing, currency, grace, and security. Resolves blockers 1 and 2.
- Independent reconciliation of the capex basis ($4.7bn total vs ~$8bn total / $4.7bn Kyrgyz segment in 2024 reporting). Resolves the cost-scope gap in §3.

## 7. IFI / donor fit

- **Which instrument fits:** none is in the structure today; the financing is fully Chinese policy-bank debt plus state equity [1]. Realistic IFI entry points are technical assistance on tariff-setting and access-charge regulation, safeguards and resettlement support on the Kyrgyz section, and corridor-coordination work under CAREC, where Kyrgyzstan's transport ministry already presents the project [2]. `Judgment`.
- **Safeguard / standards alignment:** Kyrgyzstan took responsibility for social and environmental impact assessment, land acquisition, demolition and resettlement [8]; the open compensation disputes and recorded environmental violations [5] are the live safeguard items. IFI E&S category: not applicable (no IFI facility); any TA would still need a safeguards review `[verify]`.
- **Corridor context:** the World Bank Middle Corridor study projects Caspian-route flows tripling to ~11 million tons by 2030 and finds the corridor takes about three times longer than the northern route today [3]; CKU plugs into that geography from the south via Uzbekistan–Turkmenistan–Caspian, and Uzbekistan–Europe flows are projected at 1.3 million tons by 2030 [3]. CKU is best read as a feeder that could strengthen, and partly compete with, the Kazakh Middle Corridor route [5][9]. `Judgment`.

## Limitation note

This is a bankability *screen*, not a financing decision, a credit assessment, or an approval, and not legal, financial, or investment advice. It surfaces structure, terms, and blockers for a human owner to verify and sign; it does not replace due diligence, credit analysis, or an E&S assessment. Prepared in a public-source shadow case: all sources are public, no party was contacted, and nothing here has been verified with the JV, the lenders, or any government. Every figure marked `[verify]` must be confirmed before reliance. Calculations M-001 to M-004 are deterministic decision aids over the stated inputs, not a financial model; corridor and corridor-comparison models were not run because no public tariff, cost-per-unit, or segment-capacity data exists to drive them — that absence is itself verification gap 5.
